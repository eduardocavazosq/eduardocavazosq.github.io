MY27 functional requirements from the ArchDR. These are design targets, not test results.

  • Scoped a full aero redesign toward these targets by directing the aero team's design meetings and checking every proposed change against the FSAE 2027 aero rules.
  • Triaged 6 parts of the package by build effort with the team, with go/no-go gates where they're needed, so the package stays complete even if a high-risk item like active aero (capped at 0.3 kg of added mass) is dropped.

The package manages flow front to back across 7 elements. The front wing makes about 39% of the downforce and turns air around the front tires. Endplate sides and flaps push the tire wake outward, and the nose cone and whiskers guide clean air along the body. The sidepods recover pressure lost in the front-wheel wake and feed the radiators. Floor edge strakes keep tire wake out from under the car so a larger diffuser can make more suction, and the rear wing closes it out on a new gooseneck mount that clears the diffuser exit.

The review also responds to MY26's manufacturing lessons, from wet layups with the wrong amount of epoxy to mounts not designed for load and a surface finish rough enough to hurt CFD correlation.

Functional requirements from the CoDR. These are design targets, not test results.

  • Set 23 functional requirements and broke down the 60+ FSAE 2027 rules that apply, so every flap position stays inside the front-wing envelope (no more than 700 mm ahead of the front tires or 500 mm high) and is designed to pass the inspectors' 200 N push test.
  • Compared 3 mounting locations and 2 linkage types with the team, scored two of the mounts side by side on 8 criteria from wing removal to flap slop and EE wiring, and chose a rod linkage for its lower mass, power draw, and complexity.
  • Budgeted the system at about $180 and 0.4 kg on the wing (1.42 kg with the wiring harness) with two 35 kg·cm servos, and designed it to fail safe: the flaps default to the high-downforce position, hard stops cap their travel, and a Feb 19 go/no-go keeps the wing passive if it isn't track-ready.

Active aero lets a wing change on track: more downforce in corners, less drag on straights. For MY27 the team chose the front wing, where moving a flap shifts the car's center of pressure, which helps new drivers. A servo on each side moves the flaps based on speed, steering, throttle, and brake.

MIT Motorsports hasn't run active aero on a car before. The review builds on earlier attempts: MY25's rear-wing flaps used an untested servo and a cable that bent too tightly at its pulley, and MY26's design lost more downforce than it saved in drag.

Next, the team will pick the best aero balance for each corner from a yaw moment diagram, then use CFD to find the flap angle that hits it.

  • Validated the model against the radiator's air-side and coolant-side pressure drops (93 Pa and 5.1 kPa) and its outlet temperatures, by modeling the core as coupled air and coolant porous regions between inlet and outlet ducts and tanks.
  • Showed the stock core rejects 2.89 kW against the 2.34 kW worst-case requirement, a 24% margin, which justified cutting the core 8 mm thinner to save 0.5 kg in total.
  • Set the sidepods' air requirement at 0.17 kg/s per core (at a 3.4 m/s face velocity) from the same simulation.

MY27 runs two cooling loops, one radiator per sidepod. Each core has to reject 2.34 kW at worst-case endurance, 4.68 kW in total, a provisional figure from last year's data and inverter testing.

In the hand thermal-circuit check, with 0.18 kg/s of 30 °C air and 0.10 kg/s of 60 °C water, the air side limits heat transfer (181 W/K, about half the coolant side's capacity rate). That makes the sidepods' main job getting enough air through the core.

  • Caught 5 issues in the team's mean-line turbine worksheet, 3 calculation errors and 2 design risks, by writing a MATLAB check that rebuilds the stage from the sheet's inputs and matches its velocity triangles to within 0.01%.
  • Showed the stator hub exit Mach is 0.976, over the team's 0.95 limit, not the 0.952 the sheet reports, by computing Mach at each radius from that radius's own static temperature instead of the mean.
  • Found both blade rows outside the 0.75–0.85 Zweifel loading band (stator 0.90, rotor 0.58) and the 24/27 blade counts sharing a factor of 3, a forced-response risk, then swept blade count to find 4 pairs, such as 27/20, that fix both to first order.
  • Modeled the bladed rotor in Onshape with an M8 × 1.0 threaded hub, and ran a first-pass ANSYS FEA of one Haynes 230 blade under 100–500 kPa gas loads from 27 to 702 °C: peak von Mises stress of 30.9 MPa, about 9× below the roughly 270 MPa yield at 650 °C under gas loads alone.

SET is MIT's student gas-turbine team. Its turbine is a single stage that takes 0.1285 kg/s of 1033 K gas and extracts the 24 kW that spins the compressor at 159,000 rpm. The team's mean-line worksheet sizes the stage's velocity triangles, flow path, and blade losses for a predicted 80% efficiency.

A mean-line sheet can break quietly: one cell uses the wrong temperature or radius, and every number downstream still looks plausible. The check is built to catch that, and it can be rerun as the design changes.

Worksheet check in MATLAB

The script reads each input from a named worksheet cell, recomputes the mean line, the hub-to-tip velocity triangles, the stator exit state, and the blade loading, then compares each result with the worksheet's own output.

Rotor CAD and blade FEA

  • Implementing wall-interference and blockage corrections in MATLAB, with XFOIL, to make the force and pressure data the tunnel delivers to industry, research, and student tests more accurate, and documenting the correction code in MkDocs.
  • Marked up the 108-page boundary-corrections section of Barlow, Rae & Pope's Low-Speed Wind Tunnel Testing for the tunnel's code: 275 highlights on 82 pages, sorted into procedure (55), equations that apply to the WBWT (137), and validity checks (83), cross-checked against Drela's Flight Vehicle Aerodynamics.
  • Collected ready-made unit tests for the correction code from the book's worked examples, including an 8 × 12 ft tunnel with corner fillets, the closest match to the WBWT in the book.
  • Leading my own airfoil project from CAD through CFD to a tunnel test, to compare predicted and measured lift, drag, and pressure across 10–15 angles of attack.

Walls change the flow around a model. In a closed test section like the WBWT's, the model and its wake squeeze the flow, so it speeds up around the model, and the walls cut the downwash, so lift reads high and drag reads low. Classical corrections estimate each effect separately and add them up.

The open question for the WBWT is its corner fillets. The book's image methods model a pure rectangle, so the code will need either a panel model of the real section or a measured check.

Wing elements, foam plug to prepreg

Composites, aero surfaces

Each element starts as a foam plug shaped to the airfoil and sealed with epoxy, so resin can't soak in and the cured part releases cleanly. Any flaw on the plug ends up on the wing. The elements are then laid up in prepreg, bagged, and cured.

Large composite body panel

Composites, body panels

A large panel laid up over sealed foam molds and vacuum-bagged in two sections. After the cure, the foam bonded to the inside has to be dug out and the flanges trimmed before the part fits the car.

Layup characterization

Materials testing, MIT D-Lab

The team made 6 test panels that vary fabric, ply count (3 to 5), orientation, and cure schedule, logging the carbon-to-resin ratio before and after cure; final ratios ran from roughly 50:50 to 83:17. I tested coupons in tension and compression on an Instron to check strength and stiffness against the layup requirements.

Impact attenuator crush testing

Structures testing

I supported the team's crush tests. FSAE requires the attenuator to absorb a set amount of energy without exceeding a deceleration limit, shown by test. A thin-wall tube and an aluminum honeycomb block bonded to the anti-intrusion plate were crushed on an Instron, and the area under each force–displacement curve gives the energy absorbed.

Carbon-fiber seat shell

Composites

A one-off seat laid up over a foam plug sanded to the driver's fit and sealed with epoxy. It has to hold the driver where the cockpit was designed around and survive a full event.

Battery enclosure

Composites, EV packaging

The accumulator container: a bonded carbon box with aluminum edge reinforcement, split into segments with copper foil routed between them. It has to carry the pack, meet the enclosure and isolation rules, and fit the space the chassis leaves.
