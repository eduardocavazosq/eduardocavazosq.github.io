Where it started

The brief I set myself was simple: a rear wing that is clearly better than last year's, proven in 3D CFD, not just a copy with new part numbers. Last year's wing (MY26) has five elements and weighed 4.98 kg as built.

Every design is scored the way the team's lap simulation scores the car: +13.0 points per m² of CLA, −18.0 per m² of CDA, −1 per kg, and −9.3 per inch of CG height. That keeps a design from winning on downforce alone.

The FSAE 2027 rules put the rear wing in a box: behind the head restraint, between the insides of the rear tyres, no higher than 1200 mm, and no more than 250 mm behind the rear tyres.

Step 1: 2D screening

I started in 2D because it is fast: a section through the wing isolates the element layout, and a run takes minutes. I ran 2D RANS in SU2 (k-ω SST, about 72,000 cells) at 15 m/s and moved the five elements around inside last year's endplate.

The best layout, G1, made 9% more downforce for 3% more drag (328 → 358 N per metre of span). Nearly all of the gain came from the main element and the first lower flap.

Step 4: 3D with the driver's head

The 3D model is the wing, its endplates, and a 300 mm sphere where the driver's helmet sits, because the head's wake reaches the wing. It is a half model with a symmetry plane: 1.6 to 1.8 million polyhedral cells, 12 prism layers, k-ω SST. A sweep macro swaps each design in, re-meshes, runs it, and averages the last 500 iterations. The first three designs and the final one ran at 15, 20, and 30 m/s.

The first 3D results were the turning point. The 2D gains mostly disappeared. G1's 9% became 1.9%, and after its extra mass it scored −0.08 points against MY26. The two-tier design lost to MY26 in 3D, so I dropped it. The low-drag layout F5 came out about even, and so did two more layout changes.

Every layout I tried in 3D landed within about ±0.2 points of last year's wing. The layout was not the lever.

Step 5: finding the real lever

I fitted a drag model to the 3D results: CDA ≈ profile drag + k · (CLA)². The same k, about 0.135, fit every layout. About 80% of the wing's drag is induced drag from the tip vortices, and k is set by the frame (the span and the endplates), not by the airfoils inside it.

Three endplate experiments confirmed it. Trimming the endplate to save 0.08 kg cost 1.6 points. Louvers cost 0.3. Making it 3 in deeper gained 0.64, the first change that beat MY26.

To avoid spending a night of CFD on every idea, I wrote a Trefftz-plane induced-drag model in Python: lifting lines between two endplates, solved for minimum induced drag. With one calibration constant it matched every run, then predicted the next ones.

Mount and loads

The sims have no mount, so I worked out the loads the mount has to carry and whether a gooseneck fits the new wing. At 30 m/s the wing makes 1,238 N of downforce and 388 N of drag, acting 2.85 in behind the rear axle and 35 in above the ground.

The mounting lead's truss concept collided with the new wing in three places, so I refitted it: trimmed the rear, dropped a leg that carried about 20 N, and passed the main leg through the main element in a sleeve. As a pin-jointed truss in 1/2 in × 0.049 in 6061-T6 tube it has a buckling safety factor of 2.3, a peak stress of 68 MPa, and weighs 0.35 kg per side. The same truss in 1/4 in solid bar buckles (safety factor 0.21), which is why the tube matters.

What I learned

  • 2D ranks layouts, not wings. A 9% gain in 2D became 1.9% in 3D, because on a short, wide wing the tips dominate.
  • Find the lever before optimizing. Every layout change moved the score by about ±0.2 points. The frame change moved it by 1.9.
  • Compare changes, not absolute numbers. Drag moved 18% with mesh density and up to 48% between solvers. The differences between designs on one setup were far steadier.
  • A small calibrated model saves expensive runs. The Trefftz model let me screen frame ideas in seconds and spend CFD only on the ones worth it.
  • Score the whole car. The trimmed endplate was lighter and still lost 1.6 points. Mass, CG height, and drag all go in the same number.
  • Aero and mounting are one problem. The mount moved the wing, and the wing moved the mount. Checking both against CAD early found two clearance problems before anything was built.

The problem

SET is MIT's student gas-turbine team. Our turbine is a single stage: a ring of stator vanes turns the hot gas, then a rotor spinning at 159,315 rpm takes the energy out of it to drive the compressor. The team designs it with a mean-line spreadsheet, which works out the flow angles, Mach numbers and losses at one radius using empirical loss correlations.

That left two gaps. The correlations had never been checked against CFD, and the sheet has no blade shapes, so there was nothing to run CFD on. My lead asked for a 2D simulation of one blade passage per row, compared against the sheet, plus a mesh study to prove the answers don't depend on the mesh. (Before this, I wrote a MATLAB check of the same sheet: Turbine stage check, rotor CAD, blade FEA.)

Step 1: designing the blades

The sheet gives the inlet and exit metal angles, axial chords, blade counts and the mean radius (23.6 mm). I built each profile around a parabolic camber line, the shape the sheet's own suction-surface-length formula assumes, with a 10% thickness-to-chord ratio and 0.15 mm leading- and trailing-edge radii. Then I exported each passage as a flat STEP surface: one blade, with the inlet 1 axial chord upstream and the outlet 2 downstream.

The sheet's stagger values didn't work. With them, the throats (the narrowest gap between blades, which sets the exit angle) came out at 69.9° and 59.4°, which is 2.5° too much and 4° too little turning compared with the sheet's own exit angles. Setting the stagger from the camber line puts both throats right at their metal angles.

Step 3: meshing and setup

I made the coarse meshes in ANSYS Meshing: mostly quads, 22 inflation layers on the blade starting at 2.5 µm, and one sizing control on both periodic edges so their nodes match and Fluent can link them. Every finer mesh came from splitting each cell into four inside Fluent.

Fluent setup: density-based and implicit, Roe flux, second-order everywhere, k-ω SST, Courant number ramped 2 → 5 → 10, run until the residuals reached 1e-6 and the mass flow, exit Mach and exit total pressure stopped changing.

What went wrong and how I fixed it

Most of the time on this project went into getting a run I could trust. These are the problems I hit, in order.

  • Workbench wouldn't save. A file missing from the install broke project saves, and Fluent opened from Workbench hides File → Write → Case. I saved through Fluent's console, then moved to standalone Fluent.
  • The periodic edges didn't connect. Fluent's Mesh Interfaces tool made a plain interface with 0% overlap. Its make-periodic command, which needs matching nodes on both edges, linked them.
  • A finer mesh quietly broke the run. I meshed a 48,000-cell stator separately and loaded it with Workbench's Refresh Input Data. The case file looked right, but the flow jumped about 750 m/s across the periodic edges, and runs diverged or settled on the wrong mass flow (0.76 instead of 0.84 kg/s per metre). Lowering the Courant number and changing the outlet didn't help. The fix was to stop swapping meshes and refine the working case inside Fluent, which keeps the periodic link. After that, every new mesh got a 200-iteration check: a Y-velocity contour with no strips along the edges before the long run.
  • Fluent's mass-weighted average returned 0 on the measurement line. I wrote a Python script that reads Fluent's HDF5 result files and works out the mass averages, loss, y+ and a separation check itself.
  • The rotor refined twice. The refinement rule I made turned out to be Fluent's automatic kind, so it refined twice in a row, from 31,285 to 498,960 cells. I kept it: a 16× jump is a stricter mesh test than one step.

Part 1: why the first prototype wouldn't lift

Mark 1.5 had four props sitting just above a closed 500 mm disc shell. It weighed 1,462 g and struggled to get off the ground. I built a CFD model of the whole vehicle to see where the lift was going.

  • Modeled the four props over the shell in OpenFOAM (steady RANS, k-ω SST).
  • Represented each prop with a blade-element rotor model coupled to the flow, the same idea as STAR-CCM+'s Virtual Disk.
  • Ran the props alone in open air as a baseline, then over the shell.
  • Checked the answer with a three-level mesh study and a momentum balance on the far field.

Result: the shell cancelled about 77% of the prop thrust. This finding moved the design from a closed shell to ducts through the body.

Part 2: stopping the arms from bending

With the shell off, the bare 3D-printed frame flew but wobbled and drifted. The arms were flexing. I modeled one arm as a beam and found it bent about 9 mm at hover and vibrated near 9 Hz, right where the flight controller is trying to correct.

  • Wrote a 2D beam finite-element model of the arm to compare fixes.
  • Designed a bolt-on brace that doubles as a landing leg, so the arm becomes a triangle.
  • Built the CAD in Python (CadQuery) around the existing frame and M3 hardware, then 3D printed the braces.

Result: predicted arm bend drops 98% and the first bending frequency rises from about 9 Hz to about 70 Hz. A later flight on a rigid frame flew high and steady, which confirmed stiffness was the issue.

Part 3: making sure the prop model is right

Every result depends on the rotor model, so I checked it against real test data before trusting it on a new design.

  • Photographed the real 9450 prop next to a tape measure and extracted its chord along the blade.
  • Built the twist from the 5.0 in pitch and generated airfoil data with NeuralFoil.
  • Ran the prop alone in CFD and compared it with published wind-tunnel data.
  • Added a motor model fit to the manufacturer's thrust and current data.

Result: thrust within 5% of UIUC test data and efficiency (figure of merit) within about 1% of NASA's test of the same prop.

Part 4: a new body with a duct around each prop

Instead of blowing air onto the shell, Mark 2 lets it pass straight through. Each prop sits in its own duct with a rounded inlet lip. I designed the body around the real parts: motors, props, flight controller, ESC, and battery.

  • Fully parametric CAD in Python, so the ducts resize when a part changes.
  • Motors moved to a square 450 mm layout so 240 mm props fit inside the ducts.
  • Automatic clearance checks between the shell and every electronic part.
  • Shell split into a top shell, four lower duct rings, and a hatch for printing and assembly.

  • Matched the model to the PWR datasheet on four numbers at once: 93 Pa air-side and 5.1 kPa coolant-side pressure drop, and 319.2 K air and 325.5 K coolant outlet temperatures, by fitting porous resistances and calibrating the air-to-coolant heat transfer to the 30 °C, 3.5 m/s datasheet point.

  • Set the sidepods’ air requirement at 0.17 kg/s per core (3.4 m/s face velocity) and gave the cooling lead mass-flow numbers from the same model.

Why it matters

MY27 runs two cooling loops, one radiator per sidepod. Each core has to reject 2.34 kW at worst-case endurance, 4.68 kW in total. That number is provisional, from last year’s data and inverter testing, and it moved during the project: it started near 1.56 kW, became 1.52 kW for one loop or 760 W per loop for two, and settled at 2.34 kW per core once the two-loop layout was picked.

Before any CFD I ran a hand thermal-circuit check. With 0.18 kg/s of 30 °C air and 0.10 kg/s of 60 °C water, the air side has the smaller capacity rate (181 W/K, about half the coolant side’s), so air limits how much heat the core can move.

Takeaway: the air side is the limit, so the sidepods’ main job is getting enough air through the core. Aero and cooling are the same problem here.

Building the model

I built the model in the same order every time, because skipping a step caused a real bug at least once.

  • Geometry. Imported the PWR STEP file, made a region from the tube-and-fin block, and split its front and back faces into named air inlet and outlet boundaries. A fresh import has one lumped boundary per body, so the split has to be redone on every import.
  • Physics. Steady, constant-density air with the realizable k-ε two-layer model and all-y+ wall treatment, with fluid temperature switched on from the start so total temperature exists everywhere downstream.
  • Mesh. Automated mesh on the core only (surface remesher, polyhedral, prism layers) at a 5 to 10 mm base size. Leftover CAD bodies were excluded so they didn’t trip the region assignment.
  • Air side as a porous region. Fit a viscous resistance of 689 and an inertial resistance of 95 to the datasheet’s three velocity and pressure-drop points. Both are per metre of depth, so they stay correct when the core gets thinner; I never refit them for a resize.
  • Coolant side. Duplicated the already-meshed air region (the two meshes have to be identical), switched it to laminar water as a stand-in for 50/50 glycol, and tuned its viscous resistance until 0.10 kg/s gave the datasheet’s 51 mbar (5.1 kPa).

Coupling the air and the coolant: three tries

Getting heat from the coolant into the air was the hard part. I tried three ways, and each one taught me something about what a model is actually telling you.

Try 1: a fixed heat source

I put +2.89 kW into the air and −2.89 kW into the coolant. The air outlet landed within 0.01 K of the datasheet, but that’s because I typed in the answer. It would report 2.89 kW at any core depth, which makes it useless for a resizing study, so I threw it out.

Try 2: temperature-dependent heat sources

Next I made each side a volumetric heat source written as a field function, so the air heats up toward the coolant temperature and the coolant cools toward the air temperature. I calibrated the two coefficients (121,203 and 97,128 W/m³K) so the 26 mm model hit both datasheet outlet temperatures exactly. Because a thinner core has less volume and less time for the air to pick up heat, this version responds to depth, and it’s what I used for the sweep.

Its limit is that each side relaxes toward a fixed reference temperature. Away from 26 mm the air and coolant answers drift apart (2.24 vs 1.89 kW at 18 mm). That’s expected for this shortcut, and it’s why I kept pushing on the fully coupled model.

Try 3: the dual-stream heat exchanger

STAR-CCM+ has a dual-stream heat-exchanger interface that couples the two streams properly. It refused to build at first: its “upstream parts” field needs a contact between two parts that physically touch, and my inlet faces were splits on a single body. Following Siemens’ tutorial structure exactly, I rebuilt the model with four duct stubs: air upstream and downstream, and the coolant’s top and bottom tanks. With stubs on both ends of each core, the meshes matched, the interface built, and a single heat-transfer conductance of 162 W/K reproduced the datasheet heat rejection. Other teams set the target. The problem

R1 × WORMS is designing a robot swarm for the permanently shadowed craters near the Moon's south pole. In the team's strawman, a queen lands at the crater edge with about 30 workers of about 3 kg each; the workers dive into the dark for hours and come back to dock. At 40 K, a worker's clock is its heat. I own thermal, and this study set the team's first numbers for how long a worker can stay out.

The team's figures-of-merit draft sets a 9 h threshold, about what NASA's VIPER rover was designed to spend in a shadowed region, and a 24 h goal.

Five ways to keep a worker warm

Thermal mass stores heat in the warm core and lets it cool slowly. A phase-change material (PCM) holds a fixed temperature while it freezes. Battery heaters spend stored energy as heat. Radioisotope heater units (RHUs) give about 1 W each, nonstop. Beamed or docked recharge has the queen or a rim station refill the battery. Insulation and heat switches multiply all five, because they shrink the leak instead of adding heat.

Hardware and analysis plan

Heat storage could be a finned PCM box under the battery (flown on the Apollo rover), a PCM honeycomb chassis wall, a flexible pouch around the cells, or PCM soaked into metal or graphite foam to fix wax's poor conduction. Heat switches, insulation and Kapton film heaters control where it goes. For a battery, the first PCM picks are water (334 kJ/kg at 0 °C, but it expands about 9% when it freezes) or n-dodecane (about −10 °C).

The study itself needed a one-node hand calc, a lumped network, and a ground-contact model, since leg heat leak is the biggest unknown. After a design pick come radiation view factors to the crater walls, thermal FEA for leak paths and cold-shrink stress, and CFD of how a PCM pack melts and freezes; with a prototype, the model gets tuned against cold-soak tests going from a freezer to dry ice to liquid nitrogen.

Would charging disturb the ice?

The swarm is there to study ice, so any energy we put on the ground counts. A steady radiative balance on a 40 K crater floor shows how little it takes: 0.56 W/m² (0.04% of full sunlight) pushes the floor past 60 K, where CO₂ ice stops being stable, and 7.75 W/m² pushes it past 110 K, where water ice starts to leave. A parked worker already puts about 7 W/m² on the ground under its belly, plus about 1.7 W down its legs.

Wavelengths matter too. Water ice absorbs at 1.3, 1.5 and 2.0 µm, the bands an ice spectrometer looks at, so a 1550 nm charging laser lands right on one. Microwave beaming at 2.45 GHz shares a band with our Bluetooth. Inductive charging and lasers at 0.8 to 0.98 µm hit nothing we use.

Recommendation

No single option wins. For the worker: insulation, insulated legs, 0.3 kg of PCM around the battery, and battery heaters, for about 33 h with no nuclear. For the queen: RHUs as a "heat buddy" if the team accepts nuclear, plus docking or inductive pads that recharge and rewarm workers. And a charging rule: no beams onto icy science ground, and any laser runs rim to queen at 0.8 to 0.98 µm.
